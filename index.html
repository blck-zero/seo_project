What Is DSA Starter Hub?

DSA Starter Hub is not a tutorial library and not a coding judge. It is a guided starting point that answers the question most beginners ask: "I know I need to learn DSA, but where do I start and in what order?"

We give you:

  • A topic-by-topic roadmap in the order that builds on previous concepts — from complexity basics through arrays, strings, and trees, up to an introduction to dynamic programming.
  • One recommended free resource per topic (hand-checked, with a reason we picked it) and one alternative.
  • 5–8 starter problems per topic, labelled easy or medium, each with the pattern it teaches — linked directly to the source judge (LeetCode, GeeksforGeeks, or HackerRank), never reproduced here.
  • An interview preparation checklist with a 2-week revision plan.
  • Honest answers in our FAQ about how long DSA actually takes and whether it's "hard".

Transparency note: Difficulty labels (easy/medium) are our own judgement based on how suitable a problem is for someone who has just finished reading about a topic. Your experience may differ, and that's normal.

Who Is This For?

We built DSA Starter Hub for 2nd and 3rd-year B.Tech students in India who are:

  • Starting DSA for the first time alongside college coursework.
  • Preparing for campus placement season and want a structured plan.
  • Overwhelmed by the sheer volume of tutorials, playlists, and "400-problem sheets" and just want a clear first step.

If you already solve medium/hard problems fluently, this site won't teach you anything new. Head to LeetCode or Codeforces directly.

How to Use This Site

Step 1: Understand Complexity

Start with our Complexity Basics page. You can't evaluate your own solutions without knowing Big-O.

Step 2: Follow the Roadmap

Go through the DSA Roadmap in order. Each topic links to its best free resource and starter problems.

Step 3: Practice Deliberately

Solve the starter problems for each topic before moving on. Read the pattern each problem teaches.

Step 4: Pick a Platform

Choose a practice platform and stick with it. Consistency beats platform-hopping.

Step 5: Prepare for Interviews

When you've covered the roadmap, use the interview prep checklist and the 2-week revision plan.

Step 6: Read the FAQ

Got doubts? The FAQ covers how many hours/day, which language to use, and whether DSA is "hard".

How Many Hours Per Day? How Many Problems?

This is the most common question we get, so here's our honest answer:

  • Minimum: 1–1.5 hours per day of focused study (not passive video watching) and 2–3 problems attempted (not necessarily solved).
  • Ideal: 2–3 hours per day and 3–5 problems. More than that leads to burnout for most people.
  • Timeline: If you study consistently for 3–4 months at 2 hours/day, you'll be comfortable with the topics in our roadmap. This is a rough estimate; your pace depends on your programming comfort level.

The key word is consistent. Studying 8 hours on a Sunday and nothing the rest of the week doesn't work.

Tip: Track your progress in a spreadsheet or notebook. Write down the problem name, whether you solved it, and the pattern you learned. Revisit unsolved problems after 3 days.

What This Site Is NOT

  • Not a tutorial site: We don't teach you C++ syntax or explain merge sort step-by-step. We link to the best resource that does.
  • Not a problem bank: We don't host problems. We link to judges like LeetCode, GeeksforGeeks, and HackerRank where you solve them.
  • Not ranked content: We don't promise Google rankings. This is a student project for CSET343 (SEO course), and we're evaluated on correct SEO implementation, not traffic.
  • Not personalised: We don't collect data, don't have accounts, and don't track you. No cookies, no analytics.

Topics Covered (In Order)

Each topic in our roadmap has a reason for its position in the sequence. Here's the overview:

  1. Complexity Basics — needed to evaluate every solution you'll ever write.
  2. Arrays — the simplest data structure; teaches iteration, indexing, and in-place manipulation.
  3. Strings — extends array thinking to characters; introduces pattern matching.
  4. Searching & Sorting — foundational algorithms you'll reuse inside other topics.
  5. Linked Lists — introduces pointer/reference-based thinking and dynamic memory.
  6. Stacks & Queues — LIFO/FIFO structures; used in parsing, BFS, and backtracking.
  7. Hashing — O(1) lookups; used to speed up solutions in almost every later topic.
  8. Recursion — essential for trees, graphs, and DP; builds the "break into subproblems" habit.
  9. Trees — hierarchical data; introduces DFS, BFS on non-linear structures.
  10. Graphs — generalises trees; BFS, DFS, shortest path.
  11. Intro to Dynamic Programming — combines recursion and optimisation; a capstone topic.

Why this order? Each topic uses skills from the one before it. Complexity comes first because you need it everywhere. Arrays before linked lists because contiguous memory is simpler. Recursion before trees because tree traversals are recursive. Read the full roadmap for detailed reasoning at each step.
