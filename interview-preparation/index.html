DSA Interview Preparation for Beginners — Checklist & 2-Week Plan

You've worked through the DSA Roadmap and solved the starter problems. Now it's time to prepare for your first technical interview. This page gives you a topic-wise checklist, a 2-week revision plan, and honest advice on what interviewers actually look for.

Scope: This page covers DSA interview prep only. System design, behavioural questions, and resume tips are separate topics that we don't cover here.

Interview Readiness Checklist

Before your interview, make sure you can confidently do each of these. Check off what you've done:

Foundations

  • Explain Big-O notation and analyse the time/space complexity of your own code (review complexity basics)
  • Write clean, compilable code in your chosen language without an IDE — practice on paper or a plain text editor
  • Explain the trade-offs between arrays and linked lists, stacks and queues, hash maps and sorted arrays

Topic Readiness

  • Arrays: Two-pointer, sliding window, Kadane's algorithm, prefix sums (roadmap)
  • Strings: Frequency counting, palindrome check, substring problems (roadmap)
  • Searching & Sorting: Binary search template, merge sort, quick sort partitioning (roadmap)
  • Linked Lists: Reverse, merge, cycle detection, fast-slow pointer (roadmap)
  • Stacks & Queues: Balanced brackets, monotonic stack, BFS queue (roadmap)
  • Hashing: Two Sum with map, frequency maps, prefix sum + map (roadmap)
  • Recursion: Base case writing, backtracking (subsets, permutations) (roadmap)
  • Trees: DFS (inorder, preorder, postorder), BFS, BST operations (roadmap)
  • Graphs: BFS, DFS, connected components, topological sort (roadmap)
  • DP: Recognise overlapping subproblems, memoisation, tabulation for 3-4 classic problems (roadmap)

Communication During the Interview

  • Clarify the problem before coding — ask about edge cases, input size, and constraints
  • Think aloud — explain your approach before writing code
  • Start with a brute-force solution, state its complexity, then optimise
  • Test your code with a simple example and an edge case before saying "done"
  • If stuck, explain what you've tried and what you're thinking — silence is worse than a partial answer

2-Week Revision Plan

This plan assumes you've already gone through the full roadmap at least once. If you haven't, do that first — this plan is for revision, not first-time learning. Budget 2–3 hours per day.

Week 1: Topic Revision (1 topic per day)

DayTopicWhat to Do
Day 1Arrays + StringsRe-solve 3 problems from each without looking at solutions. Focus on two-pointer and sliding window.
Day 2Searching & SortingWrite binary search from memory. Re-solve 3 problems. Practice explaining merge sort aloud.
Day 3Linked ListsImplement reverse, merge, and cycle detection from scratch. These are very common interview questions.
Day 4Stacks, Queues & HashingRe-solve Valid Parentheses, monotonic stack problems. Practice the prefix sum + hash map pattern.
Day 5Recursion & BacktrackingRe-solve Subsets and Permutations. Practice explaining the recursion tree on paper.
Day 6TreesWrite DFS and BFS traversals from memory. Re-solve 3 tree problems. Practice drawing trees.
Day 7Graphs + DPRe-solve Number of Islands and Course Schedule. Do 2 DP problems (Climbing Stairs, House Robber).

Week 2: Mixed Practice & Mock Interviews

DayActivityDetails
Day 8–9Mixed problem solvingSolve 4–5 random problems per day from different topics. Use LeetCode's "Random" button or pick from our starter set.
Day 10Weakness reviewIdentify your 2 weakest topics. Re-read the resource and solve 3 more problems in each.
Day 11–12Mock interviewsDo 2 mock interviews with a friend or use Pramp (free). Practice thinking aloud and time-boxing (20 min per problem).
Day 13Complexity reviewGo through your solved-problems list and state the time/space complexity of each. Fix any you got wrong.
Day 14Rest + light reviewDon't cram. Skim your notes, sleep well, and trust the preparation you've done.

What Interviewers Actually Look For

Based on common patterns in Indian campus placements and off-campus drives:

  1. Problem-solving approach: Can you break down a problem, identify the pattern, and work toward a solution? This matters more than memorised code.
  2. Communication: Can you explain your thinking clearly? Interviewers want to understand your reasoning, not just see your answer.
  3. Code quality: Clean variable names, handled edge cases, no unnecessary complexity. You don't need perfect code, but readable code.
  4. Complexity analysis: Can you tell the interviewer the time and space complexity without being asked?
  5. Knowing your limits: If you don't know the answer, say so honestly and describe what you've tried. Bluffing is obvious and hurts you.

Common Interview Mistakes

  • Starting to code immediately without discussing the approach — always spend 3–5 minutes talking first.
  • Not handling edge cases: Empty arrays, single elements, negative numbers, very large inputs.
  • Over-engineering: A clean O(n²) solution that works is better than a buggy O(n log n) attempt you can't finish.
  • Solving silently: The interviewer can't help you if they don't know what you're thinking.
  • Not testing: Walk through your code with a simple example before saying you're done.

Final thought: The goal of preparation is not to memorise every problem — it's to recognise patterns quickly and apply them confidently. If you've been through the roadmap and done the starter problems, you have a solid foundation. Trust it.
